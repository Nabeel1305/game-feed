25. This game idea is to ride a gigantic sandworm through a vast desert, inspired by the scale and excitement of Dune. Make the creature, world and visual design original. The player must steer the worm, maintain their balance and navigate dunes, hazards and increasingly difficult routes.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a beautiful 3D desert with towering dunes, rocky canyons, buried ruins and sandstorms. Position the camera behind and above the rider so the worm feels enormous and the route ahead stays visible.
- Controls: Use a left joystick to steer. Hold a right-side button to accelerate and release to slow down. Add a brace button for sharp turns, impacts and steep descents.
- Movement: Make the worm sweep through sand in wide, powerful curves. Give steering some weight, with enough responsiveness for players to judge and correct their course.
- Riding and balance: Show the rider leaning as the worm turns. Sharp manoeuvres destabilise them; easing the turn or bracing restores control. Warn clearly before they fall.
- Basic round: Reach a destination through a series of checkpoints. Start with a short ride across open dunes before introducing narrow passages and dangerous terrain.
- Satisfying action: Let the worm surge up dunes, plunge down slopes and briefly breach above the sand. Make these movements player-controlled through speed and approach angle.
- Interesting choices: Offer safer winding routes and faster, riskier shortcuts. Reward smooth steering and maintaining momentum.
- Hazards: Introduce exposed rocks, collapsing sand ridges and storms with visible warning signs. Keep a navigable route through every obstacle section.
- Progression: Gradually introduce larger worms with different handling, more demanding terrain and longer journeys. Teach each new challenge before combining it with others.
- Sound and music: Use deep underground rumbles, rushing sand, wind and original cinematic music that builds with speed.
- Phone vibration: Use brief pulses for breaches and landings, plus subtle feedback during sharp turns. Make intensity adjustable.
- Failure and retries: If the rider falls, restart at the latest checkpoint. Keep the restart quick.
- Replay appeal: Include timed routes, optional challenges and a relaxed free-roaming mode where players can enjoy riding across the desert.

26. This game idea is to compete in jousting tournaments. Charge towards an opponent on horseback, aim your lance and land a powerful hit while trying to stay in the saddle.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a beautiful 3D tournament arena with a long wooden barrier separating the riders, colourful banners, spectators and a castle in the background. Position the camera behind and slightly above the player.
- Controls: The horse runs forwards automatically. Drag on the left side to adjust the rider’s lean and drag on the right to aim the lance. Add a large brace button that becomes important just before contact.
- Basic round: Begin at opposite ends of the arena, charge towards each other, exchange a strike and turn around for another pass if neither rider is unseated.
- Aiming: Make the lance tip clearly visible. Let it sway with the horse’s movement in a consistent rhythm that players can learn. Start with generous aim assistance and reduce it gradually.
- Timing: Reward lowering the lance and bracing at the right moment. Bracing too early should make aiming harder; too late leaves the rider vulnerable.
- Impact: Make successful hits feel heavy through a sharp wooden crack, splintering lances, a brief camera jolt and a strong, short phone vibration. Include settings to reduce camera shake and vibration.
- Balance: Show riders rocking backwards, slipping sideways and recovering after weaker hits. A strong, well-placed strike can knock an opponent off their horse.
- Scoring: Award points for clean shield hits and lance breaks, with an outright victory for unseating the opponent. Display the scoring rules before the first match.
- Opponent variety: Give rivals distinct habits: one aims accurately but struggles to recover, another braces strongly, and another changes their lean late. Show clues through their posture.
- Progression: Start with training targets, then introduce individual opponents and tournament brackets. Increase difficulty through better rival timing and defence.
- Sound and music: Use pounding hoofbeats, rattling armour, crowd anticipation and loud reactions at impact. Play triumphant medieval-style music between rounds.
- Replay appeal: Offer instant rematches, optional slow-motion impact replays and cosmetic unlocks for armour, shields, banners and horse equipment.
- Fair failure: Clearly show where each lance struck and why a rider fell. Keep falls non-graphic and get players into the next attempt quickly.

- Scoring and retries: Score landing accuracy, controlled touchdown and optional checkpoints. After a missed landing, show the approach path and allow an immediate retry.

- Barrel riding: Let players crouch inside the curling wave. Reward maintaining the right position and emerging successfully, with the camera keeping the exit visible.

- Phone vibration: Use light pulses for landing tricks and a stronger brief pulse for wipeouts. Make vibration and camera shake optional.
- Failure and retries: Keep wipeouts playful and non-graphic. Let players catch another wave quickly, with a short explanation such as losing balance or being caught by the break.
- Replay appeal: Include short scored sessions, specific trick challenges and a relaxed endless surfing mode with no score pressure. Unlock beaches and boards with different handling.

- Interviews: Offer clear questions and unlock follow-ups when evidence is discovered. Let players present a clue to challenge a statement. Suspects should have consistent accounts, motives and knowledge.

- Variety: Include thefts, disappearances, sabotage, fraud and apparently impossible incidents. Alternate interviews, scene searches, timeline reconstruction and short evidence puzzles.
- Hints: Offer gradual help: first identify an unresolved question, then suggest a location, and only finally highlight a relevant clue.

- Mistakes: Let players revise unsupported theories. Explain contradictions through the evidence instead of simply displaying “wrong.”

32. This game idea is to compete on a colourful 3D obstacle course above water, jumping, balancing and dodging moving obstacles. Reach the finish as quickly as possible, with funny falls and quick retries.

After checking Wipeout, I’d choose these five obstacles for mobile play. The obstacle descriptions come from the show; the controls and adaptations below are my design suggestions.

Build it like this:

- Environment: Create a sunny outdoor water arena with bright padded equipment, sparkling pools, spectators and an obvious finish platform.
- Main controls: Use a left joystick for movement, a large jump button on the right and a smaller duck button. Keep these consistent throughout the course.
- Camera: Automatically choose a clear angle for each obstacle, changing smoothly while the player is on a safe platform. Always show where they need to land.

Include these five obstacle types:

1. Jumping across giant inflatable balls

The show’s “Big Balls” involves crossing four giant inflatable balls without falling into the water. 

- Place four enormous bouncing spheres between the starting and finishing platforms.
- Use the joystick to approach and aim; tap jump to leap. Holding jump briefly before release increases distance, with a visible power indicator.
- Make the balls compress under the player and spring back. Landing near the centre gives a stable bounce; landing near an edge causes sliding.
- Give players a short opportunity to recover from an awkward landing.
- Increase difficulty through spacing, height differences and gentle sideways movement.
- Why it suits mobile: Simple jumping controls, immediately understandable goals and entertaining bouncing physics.

2. Jumping over a rotating sweeper

In the show’s “Sweeper,” contestants stand on raised platforms and jump over a rotating arm that becomes harder to avoid. 

- Keep the player on a small platform while a padded arm circles towards them.
- Use the jump button as the main input. Show the approaching arm throughout its rotation.
- Begin with a steady, slow rhythm. Later introduce clearly signalled speed changes.
- Require surviving a set number of rotations to complete this section.
- For advanced variations, add a higher arm that requires ducking, with enough separation for both actions.
- Why it suits mobile: One-button timing makes it accessible, while changing patterns provide room for mastery.

3. Crossing a wall of punching obstacles

The show’s “Sucker Punch” has contestants edge along a narrow ledge while mechanical boxing gloves push out from the wall. 

- Adapt this into a padded wall above water, viewed mainly from the side.
- Move along the ledge with the joystick, stopping in safe gaps between punching mechanisms.
- Signal each punch with a brief mechanical movement, warning light and sound before it extends.
- Let players rush through an opening or wait for a safer sequence.
- Start with individual punches, then introduce patterns that players can observe and learn.
- Why it suits mobile: Stop-and-go movement creates suspense without complicated controls.

4. Running along a moving conveyor

The show’s “Dreadmill” uses a moving conveyor, with variations involving obstacles or staying on the belt. 

- Adapt it into a wide conveyor above a pool, carrying the player backwards while they run towards the exit.
- Push forwards to make progress, steer sideways around padded barriers, jump low hurdles and duck overhead bars.
- Give the player enough forward speed to finish through good movement; avoid repeated tapping to run.
- Show upcoming obstacles well in advance.
- Increase difficulty through obstacle combinations and short, signalled changes in conveyor speed.
- Why it suits mobile: Familiar running, jumping and ducking create a fast section with clear momentum.

5. Crossing rotating platforms

The show’s “Double Cross” combines rotating walkways with sweeper arms moving in the opposite direction. 

- Simplify this into a rotating cross-shaped walkway between fixed entry and exit platforms.
- Let the player watch safely, jump onto an approaching arm, move towards the centre and time their exit.
- Start with rotation alone. Add one opposing padded sweeper in later levels.
- Use an elevated camera so the movement and landing points remain readable.
- Make the platforms carry the player naturally, without requiring constant corrective movement.
- Why it suits mobile: It adds planning and moving-platform timing, giving the course variety beyond jumping over hazards.

Make the whole game engaging:

- Course structure: Teach each obstacle separately, then combine all five into courses lasting roughly two to three minutes.
- Funny falls: Use exaggerated tumbling, bouncing equipment and big splashes. Keep controls precise while the player is standing; activate loose ragdoll movement after a clear fall.
- Quick recovery: Return players to the beginning of the current obstacle within about two seconds. Add a small time penalty instead of restarting the entire course.
- Fair difficulty: Make hazards follow learnable patterns. Every fall should have a visible cause.
- Scoring: Award medals for completion time, with optional goals for fewer falls and clean landings. Show the player’s previous best as a ghost.
- Sound and music: Use upbeat instrumental music, squeaky inflatables, mechanical whooshes, splashes and cheerful crowd reactions. Keep voice lines occasional.
- Phone vibration: Add light landing pulses and stronger brief feedback for collisions, with an option to disable vibration.
- Progression: Unlock new layouts, settings and character outfits. Increase challenge through combinations and route choices.
- Relaxed mode: Offer untimed practice with unlimited retries.
- Visual identity: Give the game its own course layouts, colours, characters and presentation.
