## Attachment 1

Build the following as separate games. Each description gives you the basic idea and specific directions for making it fun and engaging.

For every game:

- Design primarily for phones, with large touch controls that do not obscure important action.
- Teach through a short playable introduction. Let the player start doing something enjoyable within the first ten seconds.
- Prioritise responsive controls, clear visual feedback and satisfying sound.
- Introduce mechanics gradually. Increase difficulty through interesting challenges rather than simply making everything faster.
- Save progress automatically and allow pausing.
- Make retries quick. Explain mistakes visually so players understand how to improve.
- Include separate music, sound effects and vibration settings.
- Use original environments and characters without real sports teams or licensed branding.

12. This game idea is to play as a cockroach searching for food inside a house. Each round, leave your crack in the wall, find food and return safely while escaping falling slippers and bug spray.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a detailed 3D environment viewed from an elevated angle. Make the house feel enormous from the cockroach’s perspective: chair legs resemble towers, crumbs are substantial objects and the space beneath a cupboard becomes a tunnel. Start in a kitchen and gradually introduce bathrooms, bedrooms and dining areas.
- Controls: Use a floating joystick on the left to move. Put a sprint button on the right. Sprint uses a short stamina bar that refills when walking or hiding. Collect food automatically by touching it. Near suitable furniture, show a contextual button for hiding underneath.
- Basic round: Begin inside the wall crack. Let the player look out before leaving. Mark a nearby food source, then require them to bring a certain amount back. Carrying food should visibly change the cockroach’s appearance.
- Food choices: Small crumbs are easy to carry. Larger pieces are worth more but slow movement slightly. Allow the player to drop a large piece if they need to escape.
- Falling slippers: Show a moving shadow and play a rising swish before impact. Give enough warning to dodge. Slippers should hit the position the player was approaching, creating opportunities to change direction.
- Bug spray: Show the human raising the can before spraying. Make the cloud visible, with safe routes around it. Spray should linger briefly and block paths rather than appearing without warning.
- Interesting decisions: Place tempting food in exposed spaces and smaller rewards along safer routes. Occasionally make the shortest return route dangerous, requiring a detour.
- Progression: Introduce one hazard at a time, then combine them. Add moving humans, brighter exposed areas and food farther from shelter.
- Sound and music: Use playful sneaking music. Make footsteps sound huge and slightly muffled. Add tiny skittering sounds, food crunches, slipper impacts and the hiss of spray. Quiet the music briefly when a threat approaches.
- Success and failure: Win by bringing the required food home. If caught, restart the short round immediately. Finish successful rounds with a satisfying view of the food being added to your stash.

13. This game idea is to play basketball purely through shooting, with satisfying aiming and increasingly interesting shot challenges.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a 3D court with the camera behind the ball, facing the hoop. Keep the basket clearly visible. Begin on a colourful neighbourhood court and unlock indoor gyms, rooftop courts and larger arenas.
- Controls: Drag backwards from the ball to set power, move sideways to adjust direction, then release to shoot. Show a short trajectory preview during early levels. Keep the gesture consistent throughout the game.
- Basic round: Give the player a sequence of shooting positions and a target number of baskets. Start close to the hoop, then introduce angled shots and longer distances.
- Shot feedback: Make clean baskets produce a crisp net sound and a brief visual celebration. Rim shots should bounce convincingly. A near miss should make it clear whether the shot was too strong, too weak or off direction.
- Interesting challenges: Include bank-shot challenges, moving shooting positions and targets that require scoring from several marked spots. Keep unusual challenges separate from ordinary basketball shooting.
- Progression: Unlock harder courts and optional challenges. Offer a relaxed practice mode with unlimited balls.
- Replay appeal: Track personal bests, consecutive baskets and clean-shot streaks. Let players retry a challenge instantly.
- Sound and music: Use light rhythmic music, realistic ball bounces, sneaker squeaks, rim impacts and net sounds. Crowd reactions should grow with a scoring streak.

14. This game idea is to play full basketball matches, including movement, passing, defending and shooting.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a landscape view with a slightly elevated sideline camera. Start with three-a-side matches on compact courts so play remains readable on a phone.
- Controls: Use a left joystick for movement. On the right, provide pass, shoot and sprint buttons. Hold shoot to fill a timing meter and release within the highlighted area. While defending, replace pass with player-switch and shoot with contest/steal.
- Passing: Tap pass to send the ball to the most suitable teammate. Drag from the pass button towards a teammate for deliberate targeting. Clearly highlight the intended receiver.
- Teammates: Make computer-controlled teammates move into space, run towards the basket and offer passing options. They should not stand still waiting for instructions.
- Attacking: Reward passing and getting open. Open shots should be easier than heavily contested attempts. Let close-range shots become layups automatically, with occasional dunks when the player has enough space and momentum.
- Defending: Reward staying between the attacker and the basket. Repeatedly pressing steal should briefly leave the defender off balance.
- Match structure: Begin with short matches of approximately two to three minutes. Include quick play and a small tournament mode.
- Progression: Introduce opponents with distinct styles: strong defence, quick passing or accurate long shots. Avoid making stronger opponents simply move unrealistically fast.
- Sound and music: Use court sounds during play and upbeat music in menus. Include whistles, ball bounces, sneaker squeaks and crowd responses.
- Replay appeal: Show a brief match summary highlighting useful actions such as assists, blocks and successful passes, alongside points.

15. This game idea is to play American football through short, exciting matches built around choosing plays and executing them.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a landscape 3D view from behind the attacking team. Keep the ball carrier obvious with a coloured ring. Before each play, zoom out enough to show the formation.
- Starting scope: Build an approachable arcade version first. Explain downs, the first-down line and scoring through play, with clear field markings.
- Play selection: Offer a small set of running and passing plays. Display their intended routes directly on the field. Introduce additional plays gradually.
- Quarterback controls: Use the left joystick to move. Place a floating button above each eligible receiver; tap it to throw to that player. Provide a separate handoff option on running plays.
- Ball-carrier controls: Once the ball is caught or handed off, control switches automatically to the carrier. Use the joystick to run, with sprint and dodge buttons on the right.
- Defence: Let the player switch defenders and use a tackle button. Computer-controlled defenders should maintain sensible coverage.
- Interesting decisions: Make the player choose between taking a safe short gain and attempting a riskier long pass. Show receiver separation clearly so the decision is readable.
- Match structure: Start with individual challenges, such as gaining a first down or scoring from near the end zone. Unlock short matches after the controls are understood.
- Progression: Give opponents different strengths and teach players to adapt their play choices.
- Sound and music: Use stadium ambience, whistles, padded impacts and strong touchdown celebrations. Keep commentary brief and infrequent.
- Replay appeal: Make successful runs feel exciting through close escapes, visible blocks and short optional replays.

16. This game idea is to play fast, accessible hockey matches. For this version, use ice hockey.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a landscape view with a slightly elevated camera. Start with three skaters and a computer-controlled goalkeeper per side on a compact rink.
- Controls: Use a left joystick for skating. Put pass, shoot and burst buttons on the right. While defending, use the same positions for switch player, poke-check and burst.
- Skating feel: Include gentle momentum and smooth turns, while keeping controls responsive. Avoid excessive sliding that makes players feel disconnected from their input.
- Shooting: Tap shoot for a quick shot. Hold briefly for a stronger shot, giving defenders time to interrupt. Aim using the joystick direction.
- Puck visibility: Give the puck a subtle outline and short motion trail. Keep it distinguishable from rink markings and player shadows.
- Team behaviour: Make teammates spread out, move towards passing lanes and position themselves for rebounds.
- Interesting action: Include rebounds, passes off the boards and quick counterattacks. A saved shot should often remain playable.
- Match structure: Use short matches with quick faceoffs. Offer first-to-three-goals as an alternative mode.
- Progression: Introduce opponents that pressure aggressively, defend tightly or rely on passing.
- Sound and music: Emphasise skate cuts, stick contact, board impacts and the goal horn. Use energetic music between matches.
- Replay appeal: Reward assists, well-timed interceptions and combinations of passes as well as goals.

17. This game idea is to play tennis, beginning with simple rallies and progressing to more demanding opponents and matches.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a 3D camera behind the player’s baseline. Keep the ball visible throughout its flight. Start on a bright practice court and unlock different court settings.
- Controls: Use a left joystick to position the player. Swipe on the right side to swing: swipe direction sets the intended shot direction, and swipe length controls power. Judge shot quality partly by timing.
- Beginner assistance: Help with small positioning corrections and slow early opponents’ shots. Gradually reduce assistance as difficulty rises.
- Serving: Let the player select a direction, toss the ball and time a swipe. Show a clear timing cue during the introduction.
- Interesting decisions: Make the player move opponents around the court. A strong shot into an occupied space should be less useful than a well-placed shot into an opening.
- Shot variety: Introduce ordinary groundstrokes first, then lobs, slices and volleys through short practice challenges.
- Opponent behaviour: Give opponents recognisable styles, such as staying at the baseline or approaching the net.
- Match structure: Offer short first-to-seven-point matches alongside longer formats.
- Sound and music: Use distinct racket impacts, footsteps and bounce sounds. Keep music subtle during rallies.
- Replay appeal: Show satisfying rally statistics and let players practise specific weaknesses without replaying an entire tournament.

18. This game idea is to act as a spy who must enter a secure location, photograph a top-secret document and escape without getting caught.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a 3D elevated view with an adjustable camera. Start in a small office building, then introduce larger locations with several rooms, floors and entrances.
- Controls: Use a left joystick to move. Provide crouch and interact buttons on the right. The interact button changes clearly according to context: open door, hide, distract or photograph.
- Basic mission: Enter the building, locate the document, take a usable photograph and return to the extraction point.
- Stealth readability: Show guard vision cones in beginner levels. Make footsteps generate visible noise indicators when relevant. Distinguish cover that blocks sight from objects that merely decorate the room.
- Guard behaviour: Guards patrol, investigate suspicious sounds and search the last place they saw the player. A brief glimpse should raise suspicion before causing full detection.
- Photography: Require the document to fit inside the camera frame. Holding steady briefly produces a clear image. Photographing while a guard approaches should create tension.
- Interesting choices: Offer several routes, such as a short exposed corridor or a longer path through side rooms. Let the player observe patrol patterns before committing.
- Gadgets: Introduce limited-use distractions and tools gradually. Give each a clear purpose rather than filling the screen with equipment.
- Progression: Add security cameras, locked rooms and moving document locations after the fundamentals are established.
- Sound and music: Use restrained suspense music, footsteps, distant conversations and subtle changes when suspicion rises.
- Replay appeal: Score successful escapes according to detection, evidence collected and optional objectives. Allow replaying missions to discover cleaner routes.

19. This game idea is to manage a city’s metro system, designing train routes that get passengers to their destinations as quickly as possible.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a clean, attractive 2D city map. Show neighbourhoods, a river, stations and recognisable destinations without unnecessary visual clutter.
- Controls: Drag between stations to create a line. Drag an existing line to reroute it. Tap a line to add an available train. Provide pause and speed controls.
- Passenger system: Display passengers as small coloured symbols showing their destination. Make waiting queues and crowded stations easy to understand at a glance.
- Basic round: Begin with a few stations and one line. New stations and passenger demand appear gradually, requiring the player to expand and adapt.
- Clear feedback: Show average waiting time, crowding and the busiest connections. When a station struggles, make the reason inspectable.
- Interesting decisions: Give the player a limited supply of trains, lines and river crossings. Let them choose between extending an existing service and creating a new connection.
- Transfers: Allow passengers to change lines automatically. Make badly arranged transfers visibly increase travel time.
- Progression: Introduce neighbourhood growth, rush-hour surges and occasional temporary closures. Warn players before major changes.
- Fair failure: Let overcrowding build over time and give clear warnings. Avoid ending a round immediately because one new station appears in an awkward place.
- Sound and music: Use calm instrumental music and gentle train sounds. Add subtle warning tones instead of constant alarms.
- Replay appeal: Offer different city layouts, fixed challenges and an endless mode. Show an animated summary of how the network performed.

20. This game idea is to work at the back of a moving bin lorry, collecting rubbish from houses. The player does not drive. This is a relaxing game with calming music, and the player cannot lose.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a pleasant 3D neighbourhood with a camera following the worker near the back of the lorry. Include gardens, different houses, trees, changing weather and warm lighting.
- Controls: Tap a highlighted bin or rubbish bag to approach it automatically. Hold the collect button to lift and empty it, then release or tap again to return it. Keep interaction simple and forgiving.

- Sound and music: Use calming instrumental music, birdsong, soft neighbourhood ambience and muted machinery. Avoid harsh reversing alarms and repetitive loud crashes.

21. This game idea is to play sumo wrestling as a huge, heavy wrestler. Grapple, push and outmanoeuvre opponents to force them out of the ring or make them touch the ground with anything other than their feet. Make weight and physical contact central to the experience, including satisfying phone vibration.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use 3D graphics with a slightly elevated camera facing the ring. Keep both wrestlers and the entire ring boundary visible. Create an atmospheric arena with spectators, warm lighting and a clearly marked clay ring.
- Character design: Make wrestlers huge and visually distinct, with convincing body weight, expressive faces and different wrestling styles. Their feet should plant firmly, and their bodies should react visibly to pressure.
- Movement controls: Use a floating joystick on the left. Move towards an opponent to apply pressure, sideways to circle them and backwards to retreat. Movement should feel heavy but respond immediately.
- Action controls: Put three large buttons on the right: push, grip and dodge. Hold grip while close to establish a hold; release it to let go. Tap push for a shove, or hold briefly for a stronger, more tiring push. Dodge makes a short sidestep and has a brief recovery period.
- Grappling: While gripping, use the joystick to drive forwards, pull backwards or turn sideways. Show a clear visual connection when a grip succeeds. A pull should be most effective against an opponent already leaning or charging forwards.
- Balance: Communicate balance through posture: leaning, slipping feet, bent knees and struggling arms. Add a simple balance indicator during the tutorial, but make the animations informative enough to understand without staring at a meter.
- Stamina: Give each wrestler a visible stamina bar. Constant pushing and gripping consume it; releasing pressure allows recovery. Low stamina makes a wrestler easier to move, creating reasons to pause, reposition and counter.
- Opening clash: Begin each bout with a short ready signal. Let the player choose between charging, holding their ground or sidestepping. Avoid long introductions between attempts.
- Opponent behaviour: Give opponents readable styles. One charges aggressively, another plants their feet and resists, and another tries to pull the player off balance. Telegraph strong actions through posture and sound.
- Interesting decisions: Make timing and positioning more important than rapid tapping. A player near the boundary should still be able to escape by turning or redirecting an overcommitted opponent.
- Physical feedback: Use short vibration pulses for heavy footsteps, a stronger pulse for the opening collision and a distinct pulse for a winning fall. Keep vibrations brief, with adjustable intensity.
- Sound and music: Emphasise deep footfalls, body impacts, feet scraping clay and crowd reactions. Use restrained rhythmic music between bouts and quieter music during wrestling so impacts stand out.
- Progression: Start with opponents who teach one mechanic at a time. Introduce combinations of styles and tournament rounds later. Keep every opponent beatable through skill rather than requiring stat upgrades.
- Replay appeal: Include quick bouts, tournaments and optional challenges such as winning with a counter or recovering from the edge. Offer cosmetic unlocks, including appearances and victory poses.
- Success and failure: Clearly show why the bout ended, with a brief optional slow-motion replay. Offer an immediate rematch.

22. This game idea is to climb rock faces, choosing handholds and footholds, managing your grip and finding a route to the top. Start with easy climbs and gradually introduce taller, more complex routes.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a 3D view from behind and slightly to the side of the climber. Keep nearby holds and the climber’s limbs visible. Begin on a short, sunny rock face, then introduce forest cliffs, coastal rocks and mountain settings.
- Camera behaviour: Follow the climber smoothly without sudden rotations. Allow the player to drag empty screen space to look around and use a route-preview button to inspect the climb. Keep camera movement separate from limb controls.
- Climbing controls: Let the player drag a hand or foot onto a reachable hold. Highlight the selected limb and suitable nearby holds. Snap onto a valid hold when released so precise finger placement is unnecessary.
- Body movement: Shift the climber’s body naturally as limbs move. Make stable positions look comfortable and stretched positions look strained. Prevent impossible limb movements with clear reach indicators.
- Starting simplicity: Early routes should mainly involve moving hands while feet reposition automatically. Introduce manual foot placement gradually, with an optional assisted mode.
- Grip and effort: Use a simple stamina meter. Hanging from the arms drains it faster; supporting weight through well-placed feet reduces the drain. Large resting holds let the player recover.
- Readable holds: Give holds consistent shapes and visual cues. Large ledges are secure, small edges are tiring, and some holds require a particular approach. Do not make ordinary-looking holds fail randomly.
- Route choices: Offer several paths. One might be longer with frequent resting spots, while another is shorter but requires difficult reaches. Let players inspect these choices before committing.
- Interesting movement: Gradually introduce sideways traverses, overhangs and carefully timed reaches. Reserve jumping between holds for later levels, using a clear preparation and release gesture.
- Fair slipping: Warn the player through shaking arms, loosening fingers and sound before grip fails. Give them a chance to move to a resting hold or improve their footing.
- Falls and checkpoints: Use a visible safety rope that catches the climber after a short fall. Resume from the latest checkpoint without a long reset. Keep falls non-graphic.
- Sense of height: Reveal the landscape gradually as the player climbs. Use distant scenery, wind and occasional downward views to make progress feel impressive. Keep strong camera effects optional.
- Sound and music: Use quiet atmospheric music, birds, wind, shoes scraping rock, breathing and equipment sounds. Increase breathing subtly when the climber is straining, then soften it at rest.
- Phone vibration: Add a light pulse when securing a hold, a brief warning pulse when slipping and a firmer pulse when the safety rope catches a fall. Avoid continuous vibration.
- Progression: Increase difficulty through route planning and new movements, not simply longer walls. Introduce each mechanic on a short climb before combining it with others.
- Replay appeal: Offer optional goals such as fewer moves, fewer falls or finding an alternative route. Include a relaxed mode with generous grip and no time pressure.
- Finishing a climb: Let the player pull onto the summit, stand up and enjoy the view. Show a short climb summary and make the next route easy to start.

23. This game idea is to challenge the player's memory by briefly showing them something, hiding it and asking them to recall or recreate what they saw.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a clean, colourful screen with large objects and minimal distractions. Give each challenge a clear visual theme, such as objects on a desk, food on a tray or windows in a building.
- Controls: Let players tap to select remembered objects, drag items back into position or tap objects in the order they appeared. Introduce each interaction separately.
- Basic round: Show a small arrangement for a few seconds, hide it, then reveal the question. Start with three objects and a simple task.
- Challenge variety: Include remembering object positions, repeating a sequence, identifying a missing item and spotting what changed in a scene.
- Clear instructions: Before showing the scene, tell players what to remember: the objects, their positions or their order. Make success depend on observation rather than guessing what the question will be.
- Progression: Gradually add more objects, longer sequences and similar-looking items. Change one difficulty factor at a time.
- Interesting details: Use memorable scenes with small stories, such as a cat beside a red cup and a biscuit on a plate. Later, ask players to reconstruct several details.
- Fair feedback: After an answer, show the original beside the player's attempt and highlight differences. Make mistakes easy to understand.
- Scoring: Reward accuracy first. Keep response-time bonuses for an optional timed mode so ordinary play allows thinking time.
- Sound and music: Use gentle background music, a soft cue when the viewing period ends and satisfying sounds for correct answers. For sequence challenges, optionally give each object a distinct musical note.
- Phone vibration: Use a light pulse for a completed answer, with an option to turn it off.
- Replay appeal: Generate fresh arrangements and track personal bests separately for each challenge type. Include mixed rounds that combine previously learned tasks.
- Modes: Offer a relaxed mode with adjustable viewing time and a challenge mode with progressively harder rounds.
- Session structure: Group five short rounds into a level, show the result and let the player immediately continue or replay.

24. This game idea is to play as a mixologist making alcohol-free mocktails. Customers order drinks, and you choose ingredients, prepare them and serve. If the customer likes the drink, you succeed; if they dislike it, you fail that order. Drinks become more complicated as you progress.

Build it like this:

- View and environment: Use a colourful 3D first-person view behind a drinks counter. Clearly display juices, fruits, syrups, herbs, ice, glasses and equipment. Let customers sit opposite you so their reactions are visible.
- Controls: Tap ingredients to select them, hold to pour and release to stop. Swipe to shake, drag a spoon in circles to stir and drag garnishes onto the glass. Make each action responsive and forgiving of small finger movements.
- Basic round: Read the order, choose a glass, measure ingredients, mix, add the finishing touches and serve. Start with one customer at a time.
- Early levels: Begin with two-ingredient drinks and visible recipe cards. Highlight equipment during the first few orders, then gradually remove guidance.
- Increasing complexity: Introduce additional ingredients, muddling fruit, crushed ice, layered drinks and more elaborate garnishes. Teach each technique before combining several in one order.
- Customer requests: Mix exact orders with descriptive requests such as "something sour, refreshing and not too sweet." Let players ask one short follow-up question to clarify preferences.
- Taste system: Give ingredients consistent effects on sweetness, sourness, bitterness and strength of flavour. Calculate customer satisfaction from the actual recipe and preparation so results feel understandable.
- Creative freedom: Accept several recipes that meet a customer's preferences. For named drinks, require the essential ingredients while allowing reasonable variation.
- Satisfying preparation: Show liquids pouring, colours blending, ice tumbling, condensation forming and garnishes settling into place. Make layered drinks visibly beautiful when prepared correctly.
- Useful feedback: Customers should explain what went wrong: "Too sweet," "Not enough lime," or "You forgot the mint." Show the relevant recipe detail so the player knows how to improve.
- Fair failure: Allow small measurement tolerances. Fail the individual order rather than resetting an entire shift, and offer a quick retry.
- Sound and music: Use relaxed lounge music, crisp ice clinks, pouring sounds, shaker rattles and gentle customer chatter.
- Phone vibration: Add light pulses when placing a glass or completing a shake, with vibration optional.
- Progression and replay: Unlock ingredients, equipment, counter decorations and new settings. Include guided recipe challenges and a free-mixing mode where players can save their own creations.
