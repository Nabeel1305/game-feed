You are responsible for reviewing and improving this collection of 111 games. Act as an experienced game developer, quality assurance tester, animator, sound designer and demanding player.

Evaluate each game from a real player’s perspective: someone opening it for the first time, learning the controls and deciding whether it is enjoyable enough to continue.

Your task is to inspect, play, identify problems, implement fixes and play again. Deliver working improvements.

1. Establish the complete inventory.
   
   Locate every game, its source files, launch route and shared dependencies. Reconcile the inventory with the expected total of 111. Identify duplicates, inaccessible games and unfinished entries.
   
   Identify the intended platform and input methods from the project. Prioritise the actual mobile experience for games intended for phones.
   
   Read applicable project instructions and understand how to run and modify the collection before making changes.

2. Maintain a persistent record of the work.
   
   Create a progress file containing one entry per game, including:
   
   - Identifier and launch location.
   - Basic idea and intended player experience.
   - Current status: unreviewed, reviewing, fixing, retesting, verified or blocked.
   - Problems found, with supporting observations.
   - Changes implemented.
   - Checks performed and their results.
   - Remaining limitations or unverified behaviour.
   
   Update it as you work. Record changes to shared systems and which games require regression checks. If the session is interrupted or context becomes limited, leave a precise continuation checkpoint and resume from it.
   
   Work in manageable batches, but continue through the complete collection. A completed batch is a progress checkpoint.

3. Actually run and interact with the games.
   
   Use the available browser, game runtime, screenshots, developer tools and interaction tools. Test the running implementation rather than judging source code alone.
   
   For each game:
   
   - Open it through the normal player entry point.
   - Experience the introduction and first attempt.
   - Learn and use its main controls.
   - Play through a meaningful sequence of its core activity.
   - Reach a success state where feasible.
   - Trigger a failure and retry where failure belongs in the design.
   - Check a more demanding situation or later stage where accessible.
   - Test pause, restart and returning to the game.
   
   Investigate anything confusing, awkward or broken. Games that fail to launch need repair before their gameplay can be assessed.
   
   Use a test mode or saved state to reach later content when appropriate, while also checking that normal progression works.

4. Be accurate about what you verified.
   
   Distinguish between something you observed during play, something you inferred from code and something you could not test.
   
   If a capability is unavailable, state that specific limitation and continue with other useful checks. Attempt to establish a working test environment using available tools.
   
   Audio quality requires listening. Real touch behaviour and phone vibration may require a device. Mark these accurately if you can only inspect their implementation.
   
   Never claim to have played, heard, felt or verified something you did not actually test.

5. Understand what each game is trying to achieve.
   
   Before judging it, write a short description of its intended experience and main repeated activity.
   
   Apply standards appropriate to the genre:
   
   - A relaxing game should feel pleasant, clear and satisfying.
   - A sports game should reward timing, positioning and readable play.
   - A puzzle should reward reasoning.
   - A driving game should offer controllable, convincing movement.
   - An action game should provide responsive decisions and fair threats.
   - A creative game should make building and experimentation easy.
   
   Preserve the identity of each game. Keep intentional 2D and 3D styles. Give each game the improvements it needs.

6. Audit the following areas in every game.
   
   Understanding and first impressions
   
   - Is the objective obvious?
   - Does the player know what they control and what actions are possible?
   - Can they begin something enjoyable quickly?
   - Are instructions readable, accurate and available when needed?
   - Are unfamiliar mechanics introduced through manageable examples?
   - Does the opening screen accurately represent the actual game?
   
   Controls
   
   - Do inputs respond promptly and consistently?
   - Are touch targets comfortable, reachable and separated?
   - Do fingers obscure the action?
   - Are aiming, movement and camera gestures distinguishable?
   - Can required simultaneous actions work together?
   - Are controls unnecessarily complicated for the task?
   - Does difficulty come from gameplay or from struggling with input?
   - Do released or interrupted touches leave actions stuck?
   - Are browser scrolling, text selection and gestures interfering?
   
   Rules, objectives and progression
   
   - Do scoring, victory, defeat, timers and progression work?
   - Are level objectives achievable?
   - Are failure conditions explained and consistent?
   - Do retries reset the necessary state?
   - Do later levels introduce meaningful variety?
   - Can a player become trapped in an unwinnable state?
   - Are displayed numbers consistent with the underlying game?
   
   Movement, physics and boundaries
   
   - Do characters, vehicles and objects move appropriately?
   - Do visible surfaces match collision surfaces?
   - Do objects clip through walls, float, jitter or become stuck?
   - Can fast movement bypass collisions?
   - Are platform edges, water and out-of-bounds areas handled correctly?
   - Do moving platforms carry their riders?
   - Are speed, acceleration, friction, gravity and bounce coherent?
   - Does behaviour remain consistent at different frame rates?
   
   Animation and interactions
   
   - Do movement animations match actual travel speed?
   - Are transitions smooth and actions readable?
   - Do characters show preparation, contact, follow-through and recovery?
   - Do feet slide or limbs bend implausibly?
   - Do balls and projectiles leave the correct location?
   - Does the visible strike coincide with the actual hit?
   - Are reactions appropriate to force and direction?
   - Are repetitive motions visibly stiff or unfinished?
   
   Graphics and visual presentation
   
   - Does the game have a coherent art style?
   - Are characters, hazards and interactive objects distinguishable?
   - Are proportions, lighting, colours and materials deliberate?
   - Are placeholder elements making the experience feel unfinished?
   - Are important details readable at phone size?
   - Are shadows, depth cues and perspective helping the player judge space?
   - Are effects obstructing the action?
   - Is visual simplicity intentional and attractive, or merely incomplete?
   
   Camera
   
   - Can the player see their character, objective and relevant hazards?
   - Can they judge distances and landing locations?
   - Does the camera lag, shake, spin or zoom excessively?
   - Does scenery block the view?
   - Can the camera pass through objects?
   - Are transitions smooth and appropriate to the activity?
   
   Sound and music
   
   - Does the music fit the game’s mood?
   - Is it repetitive, harsh, distracting or poorly looped?
   - Are sound levels balanced?
   - Do effects match actions and materials?
   - Do repeated events create unpleasant overlapping sounds?
   - Does audio stop or pause appropriately?
   - Are mute and volume controls functional and remembered?
   - Does sound begin correctly within the platform’s requirements?
   
   Vibration and physical feedback
   
   - Does vibration correspond to meaningful events?
   - Is intensity appropriate?
   - Are pulses repeated accidentally or overlapping?
   - Is a quiet activity vibrating unnecessarily?
   - Are substantial impacts missing useful feedback?
   - Can players disable vibration?
   - Does the game behave normally when haptics are unsupported?
   
   Computer-controlled behaviour
   
   - Do opponents and teammates behave sensibly?
   - Can they navigate the environment?
   - Do they react to the player and the game state?
   - Are they obeying the intended rules?
   - Are opponents challenging through understandable behaviour?
   - Do teammates contribute usefully?
   - Are there obvious exploits or repetitive behaviours that ruin play?
   
   Engagement and pacing
   
   - Is the main action enjoyable to repeat?
   - Are there meaningful decisions or opportunities to improve?
   - Is success satisfying and failure understandable?
   - Are there excessive waits, introductions or interruptions?
   - Does difficulty rise appropriately?
   - Are rewards connected to worthwhile actions?
   - Does the experience become repetitive too quickly?
   - Does the player have a reason to attempt another round?
   
   Usability and accessibility
   
   - Is text legible with adequate contrast?
   - Does the interface fit different screen sizes and notches?
   - Are menus, settings and back buttons reliable?
   - Is essential information conveyed beyond colour alone?
   - Are excessive flashes, motion and shake adjustable?
   - Does the game remain understandable with sound disabled?
   - Are relaxed or assisted modes preserved where intended?
   
   Performance and reliability
   
   - Are loading times, frame pacing and input responsiveness acceptable?
   - Are there console errors, missing assets or failed requests?
   - Does memory use grow after repeated rounds?
   - Are effects or physics unnecessarily expensive?
   - Does the game survive resizing, orientation changes and backgrounding?
   - Does restart create duplicate listeners, timers or audio?
   - Does progress save and restore correctly?
   - Does opening one game interfere with another?
   
   Treat this checklist as a starting point. Investigate other problems that emerge during play.

7. Prioritise problems by player impact.
   
   Fix problems in this order:
   
   - Launch failures, crashes, lost progress and blocked completion.
   - Broken controls, rules, collisions and action timing.
   - Confusing objectives, unfair difficulty and obstructed visibility.
   - Weak movement, poor presentation, unsuitable audio and missing feedback.
   - Additional polish and optional enhancements.
   
   Record concrete evidence. Replace vague findings such as “controls feel bad” with an observation such as “the aiming gesture also rotates the camera, so the player repeatedly loses the target.”
   
   Leave well-functioning elements intact unless there is a clear reason to change them.

8. Make substantial improvements where the game needs them.
   
   Simplify awkward interactions, improve the main action, correct physical behaviour and strengthen presentation.
   
   If a game is technically functional but dull, identify the cause. It may need better pacing, more readable opponents, meaningful choices, satisfying contact or more varied challenges.
   
   Preserve the basic idea while making the smallest coherent redesign needed. A nearly unplayable game may require more than minor adjustments.
   
   Maintain intended experiences such as relaxing play, creative freedom or no-loss modes. Add pressure or failure only when it belongs in the design.

9. Handle shared systems carefully.
   
   Identify common controls, physics, audio, saving, interface and animation code.
   
   Fix shared problems at their source when appropriate. Then test affected games, including examples with different genres and input patterns.
   
   Preserve game identifiers, launch routes, settings and existing player progress. Keep modifications reviewable and avoid unnecessary architectural rewrites.

10. Play again after implementing fixes.
    
    Repeat the exact sequence that exposed the problem. Confirm the correction in the running game.
    
    Check neighbouring behaviour too. A movement fix should not break jumping; a scoring fix should not break progression; an audio fix should not create duplicate playback.
    
    After substantial changes, test a normal round, a failure or edge case, and a restart. Use targeted automated checks where they meaningfully verify rules or prevent regressions, alongside visual and interactive testing.
    
    Continue until the significant problem is resolved or a specific external limitation prevents further progress.

11. Use clear completion criteria.
    
    Mark a game verified only when the available testing supports that:
    
    - It launches and its main activity works.
    - Controls are understandable and usable on the intended platform.
    - Core rules, boundaries and interactions behave correctly.
    - Success, failure, progression and retry work where applicable.
    - Presentation is coherent and important information is readable.
    - No known critical or major problem remains.
    - Changes have been retested.
    
    List any unverified device-specific features separately. If those features are essential to the game, keep its readiness qualified.
    
    Start with complete coverage and major repairs across all 111 games before spending disproportionate time on minor refinements to a few favourites.

12. Continue autonomously and report honestly.
    
    Make routine design and implementation decisions using the existing game’s intent. Provide concise progress updates stating how many games have been reviewed, improved, verified and blocked.
    
    Continue through the inventory without requiring me to approve each game or batch.
    
    At completion, provide:
    
    - The actual inventory count and coverage.
    - A brief per-game result.
    - The most important improvements.
    - Shared fixes and regression results.
    - Remaining issues, missing assets and device checks.
    - Clear access to the revised games.
    
    If the work is interrupted, state exactly what is complete and what remains. Keep the progress record ready for continuation.
